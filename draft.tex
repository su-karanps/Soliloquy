\begin{abstract}
Language models often answer factual questions incorrectly while appearing
confident. We ask whether models nevertheless encode an internal correctness
signal that is separable from output confidence, and whether that signal is
causally involved in answer selection. We collect short-form QA generations from
\qwen{} across five datasets, train linear probes on residual-stream activations,
compare them to log-probability and verbal-confidence baselines, and run causal
interventions by patching hidden states from correct runs into incorrect runs.
Linear probes recover correctness with strong held-out performance: best AUCs are
0.820 on SimpleQA, 0.850 on TriviaQA, 0.805 on NQ-Open, 0.913 on PopQA, and 0.821
on TruthfulQA. Verbalized confidence is consistently weaker, and its direction in
activation space is nearly orthogonal to the correctness direction. Causal
patching shows that late-layer states, especially MLP outputs, can move the
model's next-token evidence toward the correct answer, and this pattern replicates
on Qwen2.5-7B and Llama-3.1-8B. However, the effect is mostly logit-level rather
than behavioral: patched runs rarely flip to the correct final answer, and
steering along a one-dimensional probe direction does not reliably improve
correctness or confidence. These results suggest a sharp distinction between
readable correctness signals, logit-causal representations, and practically
controllable mechanisms.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Modern language models can produce fluent factual answers that are wrong. They can
also report high confidence in those answers. This mismatch creates a practical
problem for deployment and a scientific problem for interpretability. If the model
internally represents that an answer is unreliable, then hidden activations may
support better hallucination detection than surface confidence. If that
representation participates in the computation that selects the answer, then it
may also identify a causal circuit that can be intervened on. These two
possibilities are related but not equivalent.

Prior work shows that hidden states often contain truthfulness or correctness
signals that are not obvious from generated text alone
\citep{Azaria2023TheLying,Burns2024DiscoveringSupervision,Marks2024TheDatasets}.
However, a decodable signal need not be a causal signal. A linear probe may read
information that is present in the network without identifying the mechanism that
selects the answer. Even causal evidence has degrees: an activation may move the
next-token distribution without being strong enough to change the final generated
answer. These distinctions matter for interpretability and for interventions: a
useful detector of hallucination is not necessarily a useful knob for reducing
hallucination.

We study these distinctions in short-form factual QA. We ask three questions. First,
can correctness be decoded from residual-stream activations better than standard
confidence baselines? Second, is the decoded signal different from verbalized
confidence? Third, do the same activations causally affect answer selection?

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We build a layer-by-position probing benchmark for generated factual QA
    answers across five datasets, comparing internal probes to log-probability,
    entropy, margin, and verbal-confidence baselines.
    \item We show that correctness and verbalized confidence are geometrically
    distinct: their probe directions have near-zero cosine similarity across three
    model families.
    \item We use residual, component, head, same-question, and position-specific
    patching to localize a late-layer, MLP-dominated representation that causally
    affects next-token evidence.
    \item We show a negative intervention result: the same linear direction that
    predicts correctness does not provide reliable one-dimensional control over
    generation or verbal confidence.
\end{itemize}

The resulting picture is deliberately nuanced. Correctness is highly decodable
from residual-stream states, and the probe often outperforms output-confidence
baselines. Causal interventions identify late-layer states that move the model's
evidence toward the right answer. But this movement usually does not flip the
final answer, and simple directional steering along the probe direction does not
reliably make answers correct. The internal signal is readable, partly
logit-causal, and not straightforwardly behavior-controlling.

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Model confidence and calibration.}
Calibration work studies whether model probabilities track correctness
\citep{Guo2017Calibration,Kadavath2022LanguageKnow}. In language models, token
log-probabilities, entropy, margins, and self-reported confidence all provide
useful but imperfect evidence about answer quality. These quantities are attractive
because they are easy to compute and require no access to internal activations.
They are also incomplete: a model can assign high probability to a fluent but
false continuation, and a verbal confidence prompt measures what the model says
about its confidence rather than what its computation represents. TruthfulQA
highlights this distinction by showing that models can imitate common falsehoods
under adversarially selected factual questions \citep{Lin2022TruthfulQA:Falsehoods}.
Our work treats these external signals as baselines and asks when hidden states add
information beyond them.

\paragraph{Probing hidden representations.}
Linear probes are widely used to test whether a property is represented in neural
activations \citep{Alain2017UnderstandingRepresentations,Hewitt2019DesigningProbes}.
For factuality and truthfulness, several studies show that hidden states encode
whether a model's statement is true or whether it is likely to be lying
\citep{Azaria2023TheLying,Burns2024DiscoveringSupervision,Marks2024TheDatasets}.
These results motivate activation-based monitors for hallucination. At the same
time, probe accuracy alone is ambiguous: a probe may recover information that is
stored, correlated with another feature, or linearly exposed only after the
behavior has already been determined. We build on this line of work by focusing on
generated short-answer QA, using question-disjoint splits and within-question
controls, and then testing whether the same signal is causal.

\paragraph{Mechanistic interpretability and causal interventions.}
Mechanistic interpretability aims to localize computations inside model components
\citep{Olah2020ZoomIn,Elhage2021MathematicalFramework,Wang2023InterpretabilityWild}.
Activation patching and causal mediation replace activations from one run with
activations from another to test whether a representation affects behavior
\citep{Vig2020CausalMediation,Geiger2021CausalAbstractions}. We use residual,
attention-output, MLP-output, head-level, and position-specific patching to test
whether correctness information is only decodable or also involved in answer
selection. This causal framing separates our question from a pure detection task:
we are not only asking whether a hidden state predicts correctness, but whether
changing that state changes the model's evidence for the correct answer.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Task and Generation}

We evaluate short-form factual QA on SimpleQA, TriviaQA, NQ-Open, PopQA, and
TruthfulQA. The primary model is \qwen{}. We also replicate key causal and probing
results on Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct.

Most experiments use a forced-answer prompt: the model is instructed to give its
best guess and not say that it does not know. This removes abstentions and creates
a pool of confidently wrong answers. This prompt is not intended to model all
real deployments; it is an experimental stress test that makes the
confidence-correctness gap easy to observe. We also report greedy non-forced
variants for NQ-Open, TriviaQA, and TruthfulQA where available, because those
settings expose a different failure mode: the model often abstains, leaving a
smaller but more selective set of answered questions.

For each generation, we store the answer text, token log-probabilities,
token-level entropies and margins, verbalized confidence, and hidden states at
four positions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{prompt\_last}: the final prompt token before generation;
    \item \texttt{answer\_first}: the first generated answer token;
    \item \texttt{answer\_last}: the final generated answer token;
    \item \texttt{answer\_mean}: the mean over generated answer-token states.
\end{itemize}
Layer 0 denotes the token embedding. Transformer blocks are indexed thereafter.
The four positions let us distinguish information available before generation
(\texttt{prompt\_last}), at answer onset (\texttt{answer\_first}), after the answer
has been produced (\texttt{answer\_last}), and across the entire generated answer
(\texttt{answer\_mean}).

\subsection{Correctness Labels}

Answers are graded with normalized exact match, token-F1, fuzzy matching, and
substring containment against gold answers. We remove abstentions for probe and
baseline evaluation. The label is binary: correct or incorrect.
This grading rule is intentionally permissive for short answers: aliases,
minor formatting differences, and partial strings can be accepted when they match
the gold answer. Abstention detection is applied before grading so that ``I don't
know''-style outputs are not counted as incorrect factual guesses in the
non-forced setting.

\subsection{Linear Correctness Probes}

For every layer and position, we fit an L2-regularized logistic regression probe on
standardized residual-stream activations. Unless otherwise stated, probes use a
question-disjoint train/test split, so questions in the test set are not seen in
training. We report held-out AUC, accuracy, F1, log loss, and expected calibration
error. AUC is the main metric because the datasets are class-imbalanced: for
example, SimpleQA forced has many more incorrect than correct answers. The probe is
therefore evaluated as a ranker of correctness rather than only as a fixed-threshold
classifier.

For each dataset, the headline probe is selected by held-out AUC over the grid of
layers and positions. This selection can favor different positions across
datasets, which is itself informative: if correctness is most readable at
\texttt{prompt\_last}, the model's answer state is already present before
generation; if it is most readable at \texttt{answer\_last}, correctness becomes
linearly accessible only after the answer trajectory has unfolded.

\subsection{Confidence Baselines}

We compare probes against non-probe baselines computed from the generated answer:
mean log-probability, minimum log-probability, first-token entropy, mean entropy,
first-token margin, and mean margin. We also ask the model for a verbalized
confidence score from 0 to 100 after it answers. These baselines test whether the
probe is merely rediscovering uncertainty already visible in the output
distribution. The verbalized-confidence baseline is stricter: it asks whether the
model can explicitly report its own uncertainty in natural language.

We also evaluate two variants that test whether the confidence result is an
artifact of elicitation. In a joint prompt, the model generates both an answer and
a confidence number in one response. For sampled SimpleQA generations, we compute
self-consistency by majority vote over eight sampled answers. These are still
external baselines: they use only what the model says, not hidden activations.

\subsection{Causal Patching}

We test causality using activation patching. For a correct run $c$ and incorrect
run $w$, we replace an activation in $w$ with the corresponding activation from
$c$. We then measure the change in logit difference:
\[
\Delta = \left[\ell(\text{gold first token}) - \ell(\text{wrong first token})\right]_{\text{patched}}
- \left[\ell(\text{gold first token}) - \ell(\text{wrong first token})\right]_{\text{clean}}.
\]
A positive value means the patch moves the model toward the gold answer and away
from the wrong answer. We patch residual streams by layer, then localize the effect
to attention outputs, MLP outputs, individual attention heads, and token positions.
We also run a same-question control: the correct and wrong trajectories come from
different samples of the same question. In this setting the prompt is identical
across trajectories, so the control patches answer-token states rather than the
pre-generation prompt state.

This metric is deliberately local. It does not require the entire generated answer
to flip after patching; instead, it measures whether the patched state increases
the model's immediate preference for the gold first token over the originally
wrong first token. This makes the intervention sensitive to answer-selection
mechanisms while avoiding the cost and stochasticity of full regenerated samples
for every patch. We therefore use patching primarily as a localization tool: a
positive rescue effect means the activation causally changes next-token evidence,
not that the model's decoded answer necessarily changes.

\subsection{Directional Steering}

Finally, we test whether the probe direction can control generation. We add,
subtract, or project out multiples of the learned correctness direction during
generation, including at the late MLP component that patching identifies as
causally active. This tests whether a one-dimensional linear direction is a usable
intervention. The comparison is important because patching inserts a full
high-dimensional activation vector, whereas steering changes only one direction.
If steering fails while patching succeeds, the causal information is likely
distributed across a subspace or nonlinear state rather than captured by the probe
normal vector alone.

\section{Experimental Setup}

\paragraph{Models.}
The main experiments use \qwen{}, a 36-layer instruction-tuned model. Replication
experiments use Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct. We focus on
instruction-tuned models because the target behavior is realistic question
answering rather than next-token prediction on isolated statements. The 3B model
is small enough for dense layer-by-layer activation collection and patching, while
the 7B and 8B models test whether the qualitative pattern survives scale and model
family changes.

\paragraph{Datasets.}
We generate 800 SimpleQA examples, 500 TriviaQA examples, 500 NQ-Open examples,
500 PopQA examples, and 300 TruthfulQA examples under the forced-answer prompt.
For the within-question control, we sample 8 completions for each of 200 SimpleQA
questions and keep questions that have at least one correct and one incorrect
completion. The forced-answer generation totals are intentionally heterogeneous in
difficulty: SimpleQA has a low correct rate, TriviaQA and NQ-Open include more
standard factoid questions, PopQA emphasizes long-tail entities, and TruthfulQA
contains adversarially selected questions where common misconceptions are tempting.
This mix lets us test whether correctness probes transfer beyond a single QA
distribution.

\paragraph{Splits and class balance.}
Probe evaluations use held-out splits over question IDs whenever possible. This
prevents the probe from seeing the same question during training and testing. The
class balance varies substantially across datasets; for instance, SimpleQA forced
contains 83 correct and 717 incorrect answers, whereas TriviaQA forced contains
199 correct and 300 incorrect answers. We therefore report AUC as the primary
metric and use accuracy only as a secondary summary.

\paragraph{Hardware and implementation.}
Generations and patching experiments run on a single H100 80GB GPU with bf16
forward passes. Probes are trained on CPU with scikit-learn. Figures are generated
by \texttt{scripts/20\_paper\_figures.py}. Large raw artifacts, including
per-question hidden-state tensors and generations, are stored outside the Git
repository; the repository tracks the CSV summaries and figures needed to
reproduce the paper-level results.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Correctness Is Decodable From Residual-Stream States}

Table~\ref{tab:probe_baselines} summarizes the strongest probe and strongest
non-probe confidence baseline for each dataset variant. Probes outperform the best
confidence baseline on five of eight variants. The strongest gains appear on PopQA
and greedy TriviaQA. NQ-Open and forced TruthfulQA are cases where confidence
baselines remain stronger.

The failures are informative. In greedy NQ-Open, the model abstains on most
prompts, so the remaining answered examples are already filtered toward cases
where token confidence is meaningful. In forced TruthfulQA, margin and entropy
baselines are unusually strong, likely because many wrong answers are generic or
low-support continuations. The probe is therefore not a universal replacement for
output confidence; rather, it captures additional information that is sometimes
not exposed by token probabilities.

Figure~\ref{fig:probe_auc} shows the layerwise structure. The embedding layer is

states are especially informative.
This position dependence suggests that ``internal correctness'' is not a single
static feature. Some tasks expose a pre-answer state that already indicates likely
failure; others require the generated answer trajectory before correctness becomes
linearly accessible.

\subsection{The Signal Is Not Only Question Difficulty}

The within-question control isolates generations from the same question. On the
39 SimpleQA questions that produce both correct and incorrect sampled answers, the
best question-disjoint probe reaches AUC 0.724. A split that trains and tests
within the same question reaches AUC 0.989. The absolute within-question value is
partly inflated by repeated question structure, but the large gap shows that
hidden states contain answer-specific correctness information beyond topic or
question difficulty.

This control addresses a common objection to correctness probes: perhaps the probe
only learns which questions are easy. If that were the whole story, then correct
and incorrect answers to the same question should be hard to separate. Instead,
the probe separates them extremely well when question identity is shared across
train and test. We therefore interpret the signal as at least partly
trajectory-specific: it reflects the answer the model is about to give, not only
the prompt's prior difficulty.

\subsection{Verbalized Confidence Is a Weak Correctness Signal}

Verbalized confidence is the weakest predictor on every dataset we evaluate. Its
AUC is 0.602 on SimpleQA, 0.785 on TriviaQA, 0.755 on NQ-Open, 0.687 on PopQA, and
0.499 on TruthfulQA. This is not simply a calibration error in probability space:
the direction for correctness in hidden-state space is nearly orthogonal to the
direction for high verbal confidence.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.88\linewidth]{fig6_confidence_dissociation.png}
    \caption{Correctness and verbal-confidence directions are nearly orthogonal.
    The model can internally encode that an answer is likely wrong while still
    producing high verbal confidence.}
    \label{fig:confidence_dissociation}
\end{figure}

For \qwen{} on SimpleQA, the cosine between the correctness direction and the
verbal-confidence direction is 0.067. The same pattern replicates on Qwen2.5-7B
(0.041) and Llama-3.1-8B (0.017). Under forced-answer SimpleQA prompting,
incorrect-but-confident answers dominate: 61\% for Qwen2.5-3B, 85\% for Qwen2.5-7B,
and 72\% for Llama-3.1-8B.

The near-orthogonality result makes the dissociation stronger than a simple AUC
comparison. A poor verbal-confidence baseline could arise from noisy prompting or
bad calibration. Here, the hidden direction that predicts high stated confidence
is geometrically distinct from the direction that predicts correctness. The model
does not merely report a blurred version of its internal correctness estimate.

We also checked whether this was an artifact of asking for confidence in a
separate pass. In a joint prompt where the model generated both an answer and a
confidence number in one response, the result became less calibrated rather than
more. Joint and separate confidence scores had low correlation ($r=0.261$),
answers matched across formats only 17\% of the time, and the
incorrect-but-confident rate rose to 85\%. Steering the verbal-confidence
direction during the confidence-elicitation pass also failed: the model returned
near-maximal confidence across steering magnitudes. These results suggest that
the numeric confidence response is highly format-sensitive and partly a linguistic
reflex, not a stable readout of the correctness representation.

A related external baseline, self-consistency, also does not rescue this setting.
On sampled SimpleQA generations, majority vote over eight samples gives 8.0\%
accuracy, slightly below greedy accuracy. Moreover, 78.5\% of questions are never
answered correctly across all eight samples. The errors are therefore not just
sampling noise; for many questions, the model repeatedly falls into the same wrong
factual basin.

\subsection{Causal Patching Localizes a Late-Layer Bottleneck}

Decodability alone does not show causality. We therefore patch activations from
correct runs into incorrect runs. On SimpleQA with \qwen{}, residual-stream rescue
is near zero in early and middle layers, then rises sharply after layer 22 and
peaks at layer 35 with a logit-difference rescue effect of +2.76.

This pattern rules out the simplest interpretation of the probe curve. If the most
linearly decodable layer were also the primary causal site, then the patching peak
would align with the probe peak. Instead, early and middle layers can expose a
predictive feature while late layers carry the state that most directly changes
the model's next-token preference.

This logit-level effect should not be confused with answer-level correction. At
the peak SimpleQA layer, full residual patching produces a large rescue effect
(+2.76) but flips 0/60 wrong answers to correct when we regenerate and grade the
full answer. MLP-only patching flips 3/60 answers, while attention-only patching
flips none. Thus patching identifies activations that causally influence the
output distribution, but the wrong answer often remains the argmax. This
distinction helps explain why logit-level rescue and behavioral correction can
diverge.

The causal peak occurs much later than the most decodable SimpleQA layer. The

We view this as a useful caution for representation analysis. Linear decodability
can identify when information becomes readable, but not when that information is
used. Causal patching supplies the complementary measurement: where changing the
state changes the computation.

\subsection{MLP Outputs Carry Most of the Causal Effect}

At the peak rescue layer, we patch attention outputs and MLP outputs separately.
MLP patching dominates attention patching across all five Qwen2.5-3B datasets.
Table~\ref{tab:patching} gives the main numbers.

The MLP dominance is consistent across datasets despite large differences in task
difficulty and answer distribution. This does not imply that attention is
irrelevant: attention may route information into the late residual stream before
the measured layer, and individual heads can have positive effects. But at the
causal bottleneck measured by patching, MLP outputs account for most of the rescue.

Head-level localization finds a few attention heads with positive rescue effects,

This result also constrains possible circuit stories. A sparse attention-head
explanation is insufficient for the main effect; the intervention points instead
to a distributed late-layer transformation whose output is written through the MLP
path.

\subsection{Same-Question and Position Controls}

Cross-question patching could inject topic or difficulty information. To control
for this, we patch between correct and incorrect trajectories sampled from the
same question. This is a stricter but lower-power control. The largest
same-question effect is again in the late-layer region, at layer 35, but its
magnitude is small (+0.467) and not statistically robust. By contrast, the matched
cross-question control reaches +7.676.

We therefore read the same-question result cautiously. Cross-question patching is
strong evidence for where answer-relevant information can affect logits, but it
is not a clean estimate of a pure correctness signal: some of the effect is likely
donor-answer or topic-content transfer. The same-question control keeps the
late-layer localization plausible, but it also shows that the full-answer
correction story is weaker than the cross-question rescue curve alone would
suggest.

Position-specific patching shows that the final prompt token carries the strongest
causal signal. On SimpleQA, \texttt{prompt\_last} patching peaks at layer 35 with
effect +3.74. \texttt{answer\_last} also peaks at layer 35 but is smaller (+1.91),
while \texttt{answer\_first} and \texttt{answer\_mean} peak near layer 30 and are
below +0.50. The answer-selection state is therefore substantially formed before
the first answer token is generated.
This is consistent with the autoregressive computation: the final prompt position
is the state from which the first answer token is predicted. Later answer-token
states can still encode correctness, but they are partly downstream of the initial
answer selection.

\subsection{Probe Directions Are Not Reliable Steering Directions}

If the linear probe identified a simple causal direction, adding that direction
should improve answers. It does not. Prompt-level steering along the SimpleQA
correctness direction changes correct rate by only +0.010 at the best tested
setting. Late-MLP steering at the causally active layer also fails: adding,
subtracting, or projecting out the direction up to $\alpha=16$ produces no
systematic correctness improvement, with only one condition flipping 1 out of 80
examples.

This negative result is central to the paper. It shows that a direction can be a
good readout without being a good actuator. Full activation patching transfers a
high-dimensional state from a correct computation. Probe steering transfers only a
single normal vector learned for classification. The failure of steering suggests
that correctness is encoded in a richer state than the one-dimensional probe
direction captures.

This is not contradictory with patching. Patching replaces an entire activation
vector from a different computation, moving many coordinated features at once.
Steering perturbs only one learned classifier normal vector. The former can shift
the model's evidence for an answer; the latter may miss most of the state needed
to change either the answer or the model's stated confidence.

\subsection{Replication Across Models}

The main pattern replicates across model families. Qwen2.5-7B and Llama-3.1-8B
both show decodable correctness, late-layer causal rescue, and MLP-dominated
component effects. The exact layer gap varies: Qwen2.5-3B has a large gap between
probe peak and patching peak, Qwen2.5-7B has almost no gap, and Llama-3.1-8B has a
small gap.

The replication should be read qualitatively rather than as a claim of identical
circuits. Architectures, layer counts, and training distributions differ. What is
stable is the coarse phenomenon: correctness is internally readable, late
activation patches can move answer evidence, and MLP outputs carry more of the
measured causal effect than attention outputs.

\section{Discussion}

The results support three claims about internal uncertainty in language models.

First, language models encode internal correctness signals that are not reducible
to output confidence. Probes often beat token-level confidence baselines, and
verbalized confidence is consistently weak. Correctness and verbal-confidence
directions are nearly orthogonal. This suggests that the model's explicit report
of confidence is not a faithful readout of the same representation that supports
answer correctness.

Second, decodability and logit-level causality separate. In \qwen{}, correctness
is most readable around layer 15 for SimpleQA, but causal rescue peaks around
layer 35. This warns against interpreting a strong probe as a mechanistic
explanation by itself. A probe can reveal that information is present; it does not
show where the model uses that information to change its next-token evidence.

Third, logit-level influence and behavioral control also separate. Full activation
patching reliably moves next-token evidence, especially through late MLP outputs,
but this influence is usually not enough to flip the final answer. One-dimensional
steering along the probe direction does not control correctness or verbal
confidence. The probe likely reads a projection of a richer state rather than the
complete causal mechanism.

These findings have practical implications for hallucination monitoring. Internal
probes may be useful detectors, especially when verbal confidence is misleading.
However, detector directions should not automatically be treated as intervention
directions. A monitoring system and a control mechanism may require different
representations, objectives, and levels of granularity.

There are important limitations. Forced-answer prompting creates many confident
errors, but it is an artificial distribution. Some datasets have small effective
test sets after abstention filtering, especially greedy TruthfulQA. Cross-dataset
transfer is partial, so probes learn both general correctness information and
dataset-specific structure. Cross-question patching can also transfer donor
content, so we treat it as localization evidence rather than a pure correctness
intervention. Finally, the main causal metric is a first-token logit-difference
rescue score, not full answer-level behavioral correction. Future experiments
should test more natural instruction-following settings, multi-token behavioral
patching, and subspace-level interventions that preserve more of the activation
geometry than a single probe direction.

\section{Conclusion}

We investigated whether language models have an internal signal of factual
correctness and whether that signal is causal. Linear probes reveal strong
correctness information in residual-stream activations, often outperforming
confidence baselines and diverging sharply from verbalized confidence. Activation
patching shows that late-layer MLP states can causally move next-token evidence
toward the correct answer. But those shifts rarely fix the final answer, and
simple steering along a learned probe direction fails to control generation or
confidence. The resulting conclusion is not simply that models ``know'' when they
are wrong. Rather, models expose correctness information in hidden states; some of
that information lies on a causal path to answer evidence; and the causal state is
richer than a one-dimensional linear probe. Internal correctness is readable,
partly logit-causal, and not straightforwardly behavior-controlling.
